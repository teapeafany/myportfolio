A Research-Based Design Process

How do you design for lives you don't live?

Our client wanted to understand interactions between teens with chronic medical conditions, their caregivers, and healthcare systems. But we had a more fundamental question: how could we responsibly create solutions without first understanding the people we were designing for?

The Approach

Listen First, Design Later

Before sketching a single idea, we committed to understanding. Through in-depth interviews with teens living with chronic conditions, we gathered stories that revealed how they navigate identity, independence, and care.

The Presentation

Preserve Their Voices

I created an ethnographic film from interview footage, capturing tone, emotion, and nuance that written notes couldn't. It kept our team anchored in real experiences throughout design.

What We Discovered

Three powerful tensions emerged from interviews:

Teens didn't want their conditions to define them, yet weren't embarrassed either. As one participant put it: "I don't tell most people...I just want them to treat me like a normal person." Another said: "I'm definitely not embarrassed to have it...but my asthma is not who I am." They wanted control over disclosure, explaining on their terms, when relevant, without becoming "the sick kid."

Friends were described as essential sources of comfort and connection. Yet during flare-ups, the moments when support mattered most, teens were isolated. They spent difficult days alone or with parents, not wanting to burden friends or seem different. One teen stayed connected through constant texting and FaceTime, receiving photos of events she couldn't attend. Connection mattered, but genuine involvement felt out of reach.

Teens valued autonomy but relied heavily on parents during health crises. They wanted to feel cared for without being infantilized, to manage their health without it becoming their entire identity.
