What we inherited

iCAN already had a working product with an established design system and a deployed codebase.

We weren’t starting from zero — we were integrating into a living product. The existing experience centered on onboarding and baseline play; our challenge was to keep all of that working while asking how we bring kids back to the app, not only through a trial session.

Product as we joined

What we inherited was essentially a tutorial flow and core game functionality — enough for kids to learn the app and play through the basics. It did its job. The harder product question was how we bring them back to the app: turning a functional first visit into something they want to open again and again.

Design system

Components, tokens, and patterns we built on — every new surface had to stay consistent with this foundation.

The regimen

Children aged 8–18 enrolled in clinical medication trials face exhausting, high-stakes routines. Skipping doses isn’t rebellion — it’s burnout.

The product bet

The existing iCAN app had the right idea: a Tamagotchi-style virtual pet that rewards medication check-ins. Our job this semester was to make it actually feel like something kids want to open.

Feature

Embedded games — Flowerman

Kids needed reasons to open the app beyond medication reminders. I designed Flowerman — a word-guessing game embedded into the pet experience — alongside Tic-Tac-Toe and riddles as game options. Daily coin limits were introduced to keep gameplay healthy and non-addictive.

Design challenge & how we worked

Inside a live product, the hardest calls weren’t what to add — they were what not to break. Work had to feel native to a design language we didn’t author, ship cleanly each sprint, and stay responsible for trial users we couldn’t interview yet.

Weekly crits pressure-tested decisions. I reviewed pull requests with developers, caught drift between Figma and production early, and drafted a usability testing report to prep interviews with the iCAN nonprofit (in progress).
