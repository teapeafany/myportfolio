Rhythm Hop

Overview |

Illuminated floor tiles that turn hallway chaos into a beat students can follow.

Built for Herman J. Russell West End Academy as part of Georgia Tech’s Arduino Prototyping class (LMC 4813). After watching student videos and talking with a teacher over video call, our group traced the hallway noise problem to talking, yelling, and walking without rhythm—and set out to reshape that energy into structured, beat-driven movement.

Ideation

We started on Figma and explored two directions before the students voted.

Chosen · Idea one

Organizing noise through hopscotch

Unorganized sound waves with misaligned peaks feel chaotic. Inspired by hopscotch, we envisioned illuminated floor tiles that prompt students to step on specific pads at a set tempo—guiding footsteps rhythmically, cutting chaotic stomping, and pulling attention from yelling into gameplay.

Idea two

Harmonizing existing noise

Instead of preventing noise, detect the hallway’s average pitch and play a complementary chord in real time—using wave interference so the existing soundscape feels more harmonious. Students voted for hopscotch, so we moved that concept into build.

Process

Five pressure-sensitive tiles, one continuous NeoPixel strip, and game logic mapped to a song’s tempo.

01 · Mechanics

Designing the tiles

Each tile is bordered by NeoPixels and uses a velostat pressure sensor as input. Velostat is piezoresistive—resistance drops under pressure. Sandwiched between copper tape layers in a voltage divider, it gives the Arduino an analog reading: step on a tile, resistance falls, voltage shifts, and the board treats that as a trigger.

A single continuous NeoPixel strip runs through all five tiles. Because LEDs are individually addressable, we split the strip into five segments and drove each as the game’s primary feedback—active tile, countdown fade, success, and failure all read through color and animation.

02 · Sensing

Testing velostat

Cardboard prototypes had too little surface area for a usable resistance change. A wood build was too rigid for the velostat to compress. Foam tape as a compliant layer finally let the sensor stretch and settle under foot pressure.

Exposed velostat area also mattered: too large, and uneven compression crushed the baseline resistance. Masking tape over parts of the sheet shrank the active area and restored a clear resting signal.

03 · Light

Testing the LED strip

In parallel we learned NeoPixel addressing, cutting, and re-soldering. Missing the directional arrows when rejoining segments meant a lot of rewiring. Physically disconnected LEDs left in the chain also broke data for everything downstream—another debugging rabbit hole before the strip behaved.

04 · Integration

Combining sensor and LEDs

Early integration failed when upper and lower copper layers shorted and bypassed the voltage divider. Insulating the excess copper fixed the reads. Plexiglass top and bottom layers locked wiring, velostat, and LEDs in place so each tile could survive repeated stomps.

05 · Scale

Scaling to five tiles

~250 LEDs made power the next problem. Laptop USB held up; a 9V battery did not. Dropping LED brightness made portable use stable. Updating every pixel was also expensive—so we set all colors first and called strip.show() once per frame to keep latency down.

06 · Code

Game logic on the beat

Firmware grew in layers: press-to-recolor tests, per-tile color ranges to verify indexing, then the core game—a random tile lights up and fades LED-by-LED over a time window. Step in time for a green success flash; miss for red. Finally we mapped the sequence to a song’s tempo so following the installation meant walking in rhythm—the hallway-noise goal, made playable.

07 · Polish

Testing and polishing

Once all five tiles talked to the Arduino, we played the game like students would—stomping early, late, and off-target to feel the timing window. We tuned fade speed, success/fail flashes, and LED brightness until the beat was readable from a hallway stride without washing out under classroom lights. Small wiring and tape fixes kept false triggers down before we brought the set on-site.

Final product

Five illuminated hopscotch tiles driven by velostat and NeoPixels—step on the beat, quiet the chaos.

In the schematic, potentiometers stand in for the velostat sensors: both act as variable resistors in the same voltage-divider pattern, so as resistance drops under pressure the analog pin voltage rises. Code on GitHub (opens in new tab).
